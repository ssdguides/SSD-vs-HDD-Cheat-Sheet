A solid-state drive stores data in NAND flash chips with no moving parts. A hard disk drive stores data magnetically on spinning platters and reads it with a moving head. That one design difference explains almost everything else: speed, noise, shock resistance, power draw and price per terabyte.

The quick answer
Ask whether your data is hot or cold. Hot data is used constantly, like your operating system, apps, games and projects. It belongs on an SSD. Cold data is stored and seldom read, like backups, photo archives and media libraries. It belongs on an HDD, which still stores a terabyte for far less money. Neither replaces a separate backup.

Why SSDs feel so much faster
A typical NVMe SSD answers a small read in about 0.05–0.1 ms. A 7,200 RPM hard drive needs around 12 ms, because it has to move the head to the right track and wait for the right sector to rotate underneath. That’s why an HDD manages only about 100–200 random operations per second, while a good NVMe drive reaches hundreds of thousands. Sequential speed gets the headlines, but that random-access gap is what makes a PC feel instantly responsive.

What you’ll actually notice

Booting and logging in: the long disk-bound pause disappears
Opening apps and multitasking: requests overlap instead of queueing
Gaming: faster loads and less texture pop-in, though an SSD won’t raise FPS if your GPU or CPU is the bottleneck
Big file copies: the gap is smaller for one large file and much bigger for folders of small files

Cost in 2026
HDDs are still several times cheaper per terabyte, and the gap is wider than usual this year because AI data-center demand pushed flash prices up sharply. As a rule of thumb, 2 TB and 4 TB SSDs tend to be the best value per terabyte, while 1 TB and 8 TB drives are the worst. Prices change weekly, so compare equal capacities and divide price by terabytes on the day you buy.

Lifespan
Neither has a universal lifespan. HDDs age through mechanical wear, heat and impact. SSDs have finite write endurance (rated in TBW), but for typical home use that is rarely the limiting factor. Model and batch matter as much as technology.

The best setup for many people
SSD for the OS and active programs, HDD for bulk files, and a separate backup (an external drive, NAS or cloud copy). If your budget allows only one drive, make it the SSD.
